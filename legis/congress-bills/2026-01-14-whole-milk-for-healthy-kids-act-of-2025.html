Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025 ​

Listen to the summary:
educationagriculturehealthcare

Signed into law on Wednesday, January 14, 2026. Public Law 119-69, S. 222.

Headline: Schools in the national school lunch program may offer whole and reduced-fat milk alongside other milk and qualifying nondairy choices.

Why it matters: The law expands beverage options for students while maintaining federal nutrition standards and strengthening food-allergy training.

What the law does:

  • Requires participating schools to offer a variety of milk options.
  • Allows whole, reduced-fat, low-fat, or fat-free milk, including flavored, unflavored, organic, nonorganic, and lactose-free varieties.
  • Permits nutritionally equivalent nondairy beverages meeting federal standards for calcium, protein, vitamin A, and vitamin D.
  • Excludes milk fat from the federal limit on a school meal’s average saturated fat content.
  • Allows a physician, parent, or legal guardian to support a student’s need for a milk substitute.
  • Adds food-allergy prevention, recognition, and response practices to training for local school food-service personnel.

Who it affects:

  • Students and families participating in the national school lunch program.
  • Participating schools and local school food-service personnel.
  • Milk and qualifying nondairy beverage providers.

Practical impact on a regular person:

  • Students may have access to a broader range of dairy and nondairy beverages at school.
  • Families may support a student’s request for a milk substitute without relying solely on a physician.

Breakdown ​

Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025 ​

This law allows schools in the national school lunch program to offer whole, reduced-fat, low-fat, or fat-free milk, including flavored, unflavored, organic, nonorganic, and lactose-free options. It also permits nutritionally equivalent nondairy beverages and adds food-allergy prevention and response information to training for local school food-service personnel.

Key takeaways:

  • Participating schools must offer students a variety of milk options.
  • Schools may serve whole, reduced-fat, low-fat, or fat-free milk, including flavored, unflavored, organic, nonorganic, and lactose-free varieties.
  • Schools may offer nondairy beverages that meet federal nutrition standards, including required levels of calcium, protein, vitamin A, and vitamin D.
  • Milk fat does not count toward the federal limit on a school meal’s average saturated fat content.
  • A physician, parent, or legal guardian may support a student’s need for a milk substitute.
  • Training for local school food-service personnel must cover best practices for preventing, recognizing, and responding to food-related allergic reactions.
